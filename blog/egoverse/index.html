Shotwell × EgoVerse Defining Quality In Egocentric Data

Robot learning needs demonstrations that capture the variety of tasks, objects, and environments robots will encounter. Collecting those demonstrations directly on robots is expensive and difficult to scale. Egocentric data offers a way to draw on the much broader range of activity people carry out every day. This opportunity is central to the EgoVerse project.

EgoVerse brings together academic and industry contributors to build a growing collection of human demonstrations for robot learning. A collaborative approach allows the dataset to draw on different collection setups, environments, and areas of expertise. Each contributor can expand the range of behaviors available for researchers to learn from.

We see that breadth as one of the strongest reasons for collecting data collaboratively. Folding clothes, assembling components, and restocking shelves capture different skills. Even the same task can vary across people, tools, and settings. Bringing those variations together creates opportunities to study how human demonstrations can support more capable robots.

As the collection grows, it also needs a consistent way to assess quality. The number of hours tells researchers how much data exists. Understanding its usefulness requires a closer look at what those hours contain, how faithfully the motion is captured, and whether the accompanying annotations are accurate.

At Shotwell, we’re working with the EgoVerse team to understand and evaluate data from a range of contributors. We assess data quality using clear, consistent criteria for the recordings, motion tracking, and annotations.

For the video, we assess how often the demonstrator’s hands are visible. This helps establish how much of the interaction the recording captures. We also characterize whether tasks involve working at one workstation or moving between locations. That distinction describes the dataset’s coverage: a stationary assembly task and a task involving carrying objects between rooms offer different kinds of demonstrations.

For annotations, we check whether the described actions occur, with the correct objects, tools, and object counts. A label such as “preparing food” can seem reasonable in context while the actual clip shows someone wiping a tray. Comparing the description with the recording makes those mismatches visible.

We combine automated checks, VLMs, and human review to apply these criteria. Findings are connected to specific recordings and moments, giving the team concrete evidence to inspect and discuss with contributors.

Our perspective is that quality should be explicit enough for researchers and contributors to understand what was assessed and what the evidence supports. EgoVerse’s contributors help expand the supply and diversity of human demonstrations. Shotwell helps make the properties of those contributions clearer, supporting curation, feedback, and more informed decisions about which data to use for robot learning.
